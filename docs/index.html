The paper

Toward the twin prime conjecture: certificates, limits of the sieve, and a working programme

Jerry Kim

Version 0.4, 12 November 2026

This is a living working paper. It does not prove the twin prime conjecture, and it does not improve the best known bound on small gaps between primes. It records (i) the state of the problem as of the date above, (ii) what this project has verified for itself, by exact computation and by the Lean 4 kernel, now including the established bound H_1\le246 of Polymath8b, reproduced end to end through a certified lower bound for M_{50,1/25}, (iii) small results of our own: a no-go statement for a natural attempt to sharpen the pigeonhole step, on the plain and on the \varepsilon-enlarged simplex; a generating-function formula that makes certified computation with all symmetric test functions cheap; an upper bound showing that the \varepsilon-enlargement of Polymath8b cannot, by itself, reach any k\le29; numerical evidence that this upper bound is close to the limit of every dual weight depending only on t_m and t_1+\dots+t_k, and heuristic evidence that the \varepsilon-enlarged simplex stops at k=50, and (iv) a programme of original directions that later versions will test. Every statement carries one of the labels Lean-checked, Computed, Proved here, Literature, Claimed (a preprint not yet independently verified), or Open.

The problem and the honest position

Write p_n for the n-th prime and H_1=\liminf_{n\to\infty}(p_{n+1}-p_n). The twin prime conjecture is the statement H_1=2. It is Open.

A finite set \mathcal H=\{h_1<\dots<h_k\} of integers is admissible if for every prime p it misses at least one residue class modulo p. Let \mathrm{DHL}[k,2] be the statement that every admissible k-tuple has infinitely many translates n+\mathcal H containing at least two primes, and let H(k) be the least diameter h_k-h_1 of an admissible k-tuple. Then

\mathrm{DHL}[k,2]\ \Longrightarrow\ H_1\le H(k),\qquad(1)
and \mathrm{DHL}[2,2] applied to \{0,2\} is the twin prime conjecture. All progress on H_1 since 2013 consists of proving \mathrm{DHL}[k,2] for smaller k.

Why 2 is a different problem

Under the generalised Elliott–Halberstam conjecture the sieve gives H_1\le6, and Polymath8b showed that 6 cannot be improved by purely sieve-theoretic arguments: this is Selberg’s parity obstruction [14]. Lowering the bound (Track A below) and proving the conjecture (Track B) are therefore different tasks. No amount of success on Track A reaches 2.

What this project has verified for itself

The combinatorial half, checked by the Lean kernel

Our Lean development is written in core Lean 4, without Mathlib. It defines primality, the twin prime conjecture, the predicate BoundedGaps B (infinitely many pairs of distinct primes at distance at most B), admissibility, and \mathrm{DHL}[k,2]. It contains no sorry, and the only axioms it uses are propext and Quot.sound. The statement \mathrm{DHL}[k,2] is always a hypothesis, never an axiom.

Theorem 1 (Lean-checked).

  1. The twin prime conjecture is equivalent to BoundedGaps 2.

  2. If a finite Boolean check succeeds on a list \mathcal H (it misses a class modulo every m with 2\le m\le|\mathcal H|), then \mathcal H is admissible. The moduli p>|\mathcal H| are handled by a formal pigeonhole argument.

  3. For each pair (k,D) in Table 1, for (k,D)=(56,278), (57,282), (58,288), (59,300), (60,304), (61,310), (62,320), (63,324), (64,330), and for (102,578) and (105,600), there is an explicit admissible k-tuple in [0,D], and \mathrm{DHL}[k,2] implies BoundedGaps D. In particular \mathrm{DHL}[2,2] implies the twin prime conjecture.

  4. \mathrm{DHL}[k,2] implies \mathrm{DHL}[k',2] for every k'\ge k (DHL.mono: delete shifts from the tuple). So a proof of \mathrm{DHL}[j,2] with j\le k gives BoundedGaps D for each listed pair (k,D).

  5. BoundedGaps 1 is false (not_boundedGaps_one), and a pair \{0,d\} is admissible if and only if d is even (admissible_pair_iff). So 2 is the smallest bound of this form that can hold, and the twin prime conjecture is the strongest statement of the form BoundedGaps D.

The sieve constant, with exact certificates

Let \mathcal{R}_k=\{t\in[0,1]^k:\ t_1+\dots+t_k\le1\} and, for square-integrable F supported on \mathcal{R}_k,

I_k(F)=\int_{\mathcal{R}_k}F^2,\qquad J_k^{(m)}(F)=\int\left(\int F\,dt_m\right)^{2}\prod_{i\ne m}dt_i,\qquad M_k=\sup_F\frac{\sum_{m=1}^{k}J_k^{(m)}(F)}{I_k(F)}.
Maynard’s theorem [10, Prop. 4.2] states that if the primes have level of distribution \theta and \theta M_k/2>1 then \mathrm{DHL}[k,2] holds. With Bombieri–Vinogradov (\theta<1/2) the condition is M_k>4.

We take F=\sum_{a+2b\le d}c_{a,b}(1-P_1)^aP_2^b with P_j=\sum_it_i^j, as in [10, §8]. All integrals reduce to

\int_{\mathcal{R}_k}(1-P_1)^AP_2^B\,dt=\frac{A!}{(k+A+2B)!}\,G_k(B),\qquad \sum_{B\ge0}G_k(B)\frac{x^B}{B!}=\left(\sum_{b\ge0}\frac{(2b)!}{b!}x^b\right)^{k},
which follows from the Dirichlet integral. The two quadratic forms are then exact rational matrices. We compute an approximate top eigenvector in floating point, round it to rationals, and evaluate the Rayleigh quotient in exact arithmetic; the last step is a rigorous lower bound whatever happened before it.

Theorem 2 (Computed, exact rational arithmetic). With F a polynomial in 1-P_1 and P_2 only,

M_{105}\ge4.00206976,\qquad M_{102}\ge4.00271343,\qquad M_{5}\ge2.00410541 .

The first value reproduces Maynard’s M_{105}>4 with d=11 (42 coefficients). The third gives \mathrm{DHL}[5,2] and H_1\le12 under the Elliott–Halberstam conjecture, again reproducing [10]. For d=11,15,19 the least k at which this family certifies M_k>4 is 105,102,102, and at k=101 it gives only 3.99931 (d=19): polynomials in P_1,P_2 alone stop at k=102.

All symmetric test functions

To go further we allow every symmetric polynomial. Since P_1,\dots,P_k generate the symmetric polynomials, the functions

(1-P_1)^a\,P^{b}:=(1-P_1)^a\prod_{j\ge2}P_j^{b_j},\qquad a+w(b)\le d,\quad w(b)=\sum_j jb_j,
span all symmetric polynomials of degree at most d (for k\ge d). The integrals have a closed form that we have not seen stated in this way (Proved here; possibly folklore).

Lemma 3. Let X_1,\dots,X_k be independent exponential random variables of mean 1 and E_k(b)=\mathbb E\prod_jP_j(X)^{b_j}. Then

\int_{\mathcal{R}_k}(1-P_1)^AP^{b}\,dt=\frac{A!}{(k+A+w(b))!}\,E_k(b), \qquad \sum_b E_k(b)\frac{s^b}{b!}=\left(\sum_{c}w(c)!\,\frac{s^c}{c!}\right)^{k},
where s^b=\prod_js_j^{b_j} and b!=\prod_jb_j!. In particular each E_k(b) is a positive integer, computed exactly by multiplying exponential generating functions.

Proof. For a monomial, the Dirichlet integral gives \int_{\mathcal{R}_k}(1-P_1)^At^\alpha\,dt=A!\,\alpha!/(k+A+|\alpha|)!, and \alpha!=\mathbb EX^\alpha. Summing over the monomials of P^b, all of degree w(b), gives the first identity. For the second, \mathbb E\exp(\sum_js_jP_j(X))=\left(\mathbb E\exp(\sum_js_jX_1^j)\right)^k by independence, and \mathbb E\exp(\sum_js_jX_1^j)=\sum_c\mathbb E X_1^{w(c)}\,s^c/c!=\sum_cw(c)!\,s^c/c!, as formal power series. ◻

The J-functional reduces to the same lemma in k-1 variables: with P_j=P_j'+t_k^j,

\int_0^{1-P_1'}(1-P_1)^aP^b\,dt_k=\sum_{e\le b}\binom{b}{e} \frac{a!\,w(e)!}{(a+w(e)+1)!}\,(1-P_1')^{a+w(e)+1}P'^{\,b-e}.
So both Gram matrices are exact rational matrices. We find an approximate top eigenvector by inverse iteration with a fixed shift above \frac{k}{k-1}\log k, which can only converge to the top eigenvalue. We take its floating-point midpoints as exact dyadic rationals and evaluate the Rayleigh quotient exactly. The working precision of the eigenvector matters only for speed. A separate script, code/verify_cert.py, rebuilds both Gram matrices from scratch and re-evaluates each stored coefficient vector in exact arithmetic.

Polymath8b’s \varepsilon-enlarged simplex [14, Thm. 3.12] (Literature; we re-read the statement in the arXiv text for this version) takes F supported on (1+\varepsilon)\mathcal{R}_k and integrates the square of each marginal \int F\,dt_m only over (1-\varepsilon)\mathcal{R}_{k-1}. Call the resulting constant M_{k,\varepsilon}. If \mathrm{EH}[\theta] holds with 1+\varepsilon<1/\theta and M_{k,\varepsilon}>2/\theta, then \mathrm{DHL}[k,2] holds. Bombieri–Vinogradov gives every \theta<1/2, so for every fixed \varepsilon<1 the condition is M_{k,\varepsilon}>4.

We extended our pipeline to this functional, following the route that [14, §7.2] describes in words. Put L=1+\varepsilon, \tau=1-\varepsilon and use the basis (L-P_1)^aP^b (b supported on even j). The following identities are elementary (Proved here): the substitution t=Ls and Lemma 3 give

\int_{L\mathcal{R}_k}(L-P_1)^AP^b\,dt=L^{k+A+w(b)}\,\frac{A!}{(k+A+w(b))!}\,E_k(b);
the inner integral over t_m\in[0,L-P_1'] is the formula displayed after Lemma 3 with 1-P_1' replaced by L-P_1'; and on \tau\mathcal{R}_{k-1} we write L-P_1'=2\varepsilon+(\tau-P_1'), so that
\int_{\tau\mathcal{R}_{k-1}}(L-P_1')^AP'^{\,B}=\sum_{i=0}^{A}\binom Ai(2\varepsilon)^{A-i}\, \tau^{k-1+i+w(B)}\,\frac{i!}{(k-1+i+w(B))!}\,E_{k-1}(B).
For rational \varepsilon both Gram matrices are again exact rational matrices. We checked the code against hand computation for k=2,3 and F\in\{1,L-P_1\}, and against direct iterated symbolic integration (sympy, without Lemma 3 or the binomial rewrite) of every Gram entry for (k,d,\varepsilon)=(3,3,1/5) and (4,4,1/25): no mismatch (code/check_eps_sympy.py). At \varepsilon=0 it is the old code path (an old certificate re-verifies unchanged). Results of our own: rigidity of the pigeonhole step

Every bound above ends with the same step: if the weighted average number of primes among n+h_1,\dots,n+h_k exceeds 1, two of them are prime, and they are at most h_k-h_1 apart. A natural hope is to do better with unequal weights. Fix D, and give each position a weight c_i\ge0. Call a set of positions D-separated if any two of its shifts differ by more than D, and let \alpha_D(c) be the largest total weight of a D-separated set. If no two primes among the n+h_i are within D, then \sum_ic_i\mathbf 1_{n+h_i\text{ prime}}\le\alpha_D(c). Writing

M_k[c]=\sup_F\frac{\sum_{m}c_mJ_k^{(m)}(F)}{I_k(F)},
the sieve therefore yields H_1\le D as soon as \tfrac{\theta}{2}M_k[c]>\alpha_D(c). For c=(1,\dots,1) and D=h_k-h_1 this is the usual criterion. Could a cleverer c certify some D<h_k-h_1 that no sub-tuple certifies?

Proposition 8 (Proved here; possibly folklore). Let \omega be the largest number of shifts h_i contained in an interval of length D. Then for every c\ge0, not identically zero,

\frac{M_k[c]}{\alpha_D(c)}\le M_\omega .
So the weighted criterion never certifies more than the standard criterion applied to the best sub-tuple of diameter at most D.

Proof. Let G be the graph on \{1,\dots,k\} joining i\ne j when |h_i-h_j|\le D. Its stable sets are the D-separated sets and its cliques are the sets of shifts lying in an interval of length D. Normalise \alpha_D(c)=1, so c lies in

P=\{c\ge0:\ c(S)\le1\text{ for every stable set }S\}.
The graph G is a unit interval graph, hence perfect, and so is its complement by the perfect graph theorem [9]. For a perfect graph the polytope cut out by the clique inequalities is the convex hull of the indicator vectors of its stable sets [5]. Applying this to the complement of G gives
P=\operatorname{conv}\{\mathbf 1_K:\ K\text{ a clique of }G\}.

The map c\mapsto M_k[c] is a supremum of linear functionals, hence convex, and therefore M_k[c]\le\max_KM_k[\mathbf 1_K]. It remains to show M_k[\mathbf 1_K]\le M_{j} for |K|=j; this suffices because M_j is non-decreasing in j (adjoin a variable and concentrate F near its zero set). Write t=(s,u) with s the coordinates in K and u the others, put \sigma(u)=1-\sum u\ge0, and let F_u=F(\cdot,u), a function on the scaled simplex \sigma(u)\mathcal{R}_j. For m\in K we have J_k^{(m)}(F)=\int J_j^{(m)}(F_u)\,du, and I_k(F)=\int I_j(F_u)\,du. Rescaling F_u to \mathcal{R}_j multiplies I_j by \sigma^{j} and each J_j^{(m)} by \sigma^{j+1}, so

\sum_{m\in K}J_j^{(m)}(F_u)\ \le\ \sigma(u)\,M_j\,I_j(F_u)\ \le\ M_j\,I_j(F_u).
Integrating over u gives the claim. ◻

Remark 9 (Computed, consistency check for k=3). With unrestricted polynomials of degree 6 on \mathcal{R}_3 we find the lower bounds M_3[(1,1,1)]\ge1.6464, M_3[(1,1,0)]\ge1.3579 and M_3[(\tfrac12,1,\tfrac12)]\ge1.1604, against M_2=1/(1-W(1/e))=1.3859\ldots . The same rigidity on the enlarged simplex

The \varepsilon-enlarged simplex of Polymath8b [14, Thm. 3.12] takes F supported on (1+\varepsilon)\mathcal{R}_k and restricts the outer integral of each J^{(m)} to (1-\varepsilon)\mathcal{R}_{k-1}. Call the resulting constant M_{k,\varepsilon}. The criterion M_{k,\varepsilon}>2/\theta is valid in two cases (Literature): under \mathrm{EH}[\theta] when 1+\varepsilon<1/\theta, and under \mathrm{GEH}[\theta] when \varepsilon<1/(k-1). With Bombieri–Vinogradov the first case allows every \varepsilon<1, and Polymath8b’s H_1\le246 uses M_{50,1/25}>4.0043 [14, Thm. 3.13]. Unequal weights are no more useful there.

Proposition 10 (Proved here; possibly folklore). For 0\le\varepsilon<1 and every weight c\ge0, c\ne0, M_{k,\varepsilon}[c]/\alpha_D(c)\le M_{\omega,\varepsilon}, with equality for the indicator of a largest clique. Since the conditions on \varepsilon above do not get harder as k decreases, the weighted criterion never certifies more than the standard one for the best sub-tuple of diameter at most D.

Proof. As for Proposition 8, it suffices to show \sum_{m\in K}J_{k,\varepsilon}^{(m)}(F)\le M_{j,\varepsilon}I_k(F) for a clique K of size j, together with M_{j,\varepsilon}\le M_{\omega,\varepsilon}. Fix the coordinates u outside K, with |u|\le1-\varepsilon (other slices do not meet the J-region). The slice F_u is supported on (1+\varepsilon-|u|)\mathcal{R}_j, and its J-region is (1-\varepsilon-|u|)\mathcal{R}_{j-1}. Put \mu=(1+\varepsilon-|u|)/(1+\varepsilon)\le1 and H(x)=F_u(\mu x), which is supported on (1+\varepsilon)\mathcal{R}_j. Then I_j(F_u)=\mu^jI_j(H), and each slice J-term equals \mu^{j+1} times the integral of (\int H\,dx_m)^2 over |x'|\le(1-\varepsilon-|u|)/\mu. Since

\frac{(1-\varepsilon-|u|)(1+\varepsilon)}{1+\varepsilon-|u|}\le1-\varepsilon \iff -|u|(1+\varepsilon)\le-|u|(1-\varepsilon),
that region lies inside (1-\varepsilon)\mathcal{R}_{j-1}, so the slice contributes at most \mu^{j+1}M_{j,\varepsilon}I_j(H)=\mu M_{j,\varepsilon}I_j(F_u). For j=1 use Cauchy–Schwarz, which gives M_{1,\varepsilon}=1+\varepsilon. Integrate over u and use \mu\le1. Monotonicity in j follows by embedding: take F(s,u)=H(s/\mu_\delta)\mathbf 1_{[0,\delta]^{k-j}}(u) with \mu_\delta=1-(k-j)\delta/(1+\varepsilon) and let \delta\to0. ◻

Slicing naively gives each slice its own parameter \varepsilon'=\varepsilon/(1-|u|)\ge\varepsilon, which would only bound the weighted constant by \sup_{\varepsilon'\ge\varepsilon}M_{j,\varepsilon'}, a weaker statement. The single rescaling by \mu above avoids that loss.

Results of our own: how far the \varepsilon-enlargement alone can go

For the plain simplex the bound M_k\le\frac{k}{k-1}\log k shows where the method stops (k\ge51). This section starts the same analysis for the \varepsilon-enlarged simplex. For 0<\tau\le L put

M_k(L,\tau)=\sup_F\frac{\sum_{m=1}^k\int_{\tau\mathcal{R}_{k-1}}\left(\int_0^\infty F\,dt_m\right)^2\prod_{i\ne m}dt_i}{\int F^2},
the supremum over square-integrable F\ne0 supported on L\mathcal{R}_k. Then M_{k,\varepsilon}=M_k(1+\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon) and M_k=M_k(1,1). The quantity M_k(L,\tau) is non-decreasing in L and in \tau, satisfies M_k(cL,c\tau)=c\,M_k(L,\tau), and is non-decreasing in k by the embedding used in the proof of Proposition 10. Write S=t_1+\dots+t_k and call the index m active at t if S-t_m\le\tau.

Lemma 13 (Proved here; it is [14, Lemma 6.1] with the region of the marginals kept). Let \psi_1,\dots,\psi_k be measurable functions on L\mathcal{R}_k, positive almost everywhere, such that for almost every t'\in\tau\mathcal{R}_{k-1} and every m

\int_0^{L-|t'|}\frac{du}{\psi_m(t',u)}\le1,
where (t',u) is the point whose m-th coordinate is u and whose other coordinates are t'. Then
M_k(L,\tau)\le\operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{t\in L\mathcal{R}_k}\ \sum_{m\text{ active at }t}\psi_m(t).

Proof. For t'\in\tau\mathcal{R}_{k-1} the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives

\left(\int F\,dt_m\right)^2\le\int\frac{dt_m}{\psi_m}\cdot\int\psi_mF^2\,dt_m\le\int\psi_mF^2\,dt_m .
Integrating over t'\in\tau\mathcal{R}_{k-1} gives the integral of \psi_mF^2 over the set where m is active. Sum over m. ◻

Proposition 14 (Proved here; elementary, possibly folklore). For k\ge2 and 0\le\varepsilon<1,

M_{k,\varepsilon}\le(1+\varepsilon)M_k\le(1+\varepsilon)\frac{k}{k-1}\log k .
Conversely, for 0\le\varepsilon\le M_k/(4k),
M_{k,\varepsilon}\ge(1+\varepsilon)M_k-\frac{64k^2}{M_k}\,\varepsilon^2 .
In particular \lim_{\varepsilon\to0^+}(M_{k,\varepsilon}-M_k)/\varepsilon=M_k.

Proof. By monotonicity in \tau and scaling, M_k(1+\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon)\le M_k(1+\varepsilon,1+\varepsilon)=(1+\varepsilon)M_k, and M_k\le\frac{k}{k-1}\log k is [14, Cor. 6.4].

For the lower bound, scaling gives M_{k,\varepsilon}=(1+\varepsilon)M_k(1,\rho) with \rho=(1-\varepsilon)/(1+\varepsilon). Put \delta=k(1-\rho) and let F on \mathcal{R}_k satisfy \sum_mJ_k^{(m)}(F)\ge(M_k-\eta)I_k(F). Split F=F_1+F_2 with F_2=F\mathbf 1_{S>\rho}, and let x=I_k(F_2)/I_k(F). Two facts follow from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality on segments of length at most 1-\rho. First, a fiber of F_2 has length at most 1-\rho, so \sum_mJ_k^{(m)}(F_2)\le\delta\,I_k(F_2). Second, a fiber over t' with |t'|>\rho lies in \{S>\rho\} and has length at most 1-\rho, so restricting the marginals of F to \rho\mathcal{R}_{k-1} loses at most \delta\,I_k(F_2). By the triangle inequality for F\mapsto(\int F\,dt_m)_{m\le k} and the first fact,

M_k-\eta\le\left(\sqrt{M_k(1-x)}+\sqrt{\delta x}\right)^2\le M_k(1-x)+2\sqrt{M_k\delta x}+\delta x .
This is a quadratic inequality in \sqrt x. If \delta\le M_k/2 it gives x\le(16\delta+8\eta)/M_k. By the second fact the quotient of F for M_k(1,\rho) is at least M_k-\eta-\delta x. Letting \eta\to0 we get M_k(1,\rho)\ge M_k-16\delta^2/M_k. Finally \delta=2k\varepsilon/(1+\varepsilon), and \delta\le M_k/2 when \varepsilon\le M_k/(4k). ◻

So for fixed k the factor 1+\varepsilon is exact to first order, and no dual argument can improve it there. Any saving is an effect of large k: it needs \varepsilon of size about 1/k or more, where the shell \rho<\sigma\le1 carries most of the mass. The first inequality already restricts the parameter: with Bombieri–Vinogradov the criterion M_{k,\varepsilon}>4 forces \varepsilon>\varepsilon_0(k)=\frac{4(k-1)}{k\log k}-1 (Computed):

Theorem 15 (Proved here for the inequality; Computed, interval arithmetic, for the numerical statement). Let k\ge2, 0<\tau<s_1\le L and a\ge s_1. Then

M_k(L,\tau)\le\sup_{0\le\sigma\le\tau}W(\sigma),\qquad W(\sigma)=\frac{ka}{k-1}\log\left(1+\frac{(k-1)(s_1-\sigma)}{a-\sigma}\right)+L-s_1+\sigma\log\frac{L-\sigma}{s_1-\sigma}.
Consequently M_{k,\varepsilon}<3.99 for every k\le29 and every 0\le\varepsilon<1. In particular the criterion of [14, Thm. 3.12] on the \varepsilon-enlarged simplex cannot give \mathrm{DHL}[k,2] for any k\le29 from the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem, nor from any level of distribution \theta\le0.501, whatever the value of \varepsilon.

Proof. Fix \Lambda>0, put \lambda=\Lambda/(ka) and take the two-zone weight

\psi_m(t)=\lambda\,(a-S+kt_m)\quad(S\le s_1),\qquad \psi_m(t)=\Lambda\,\frac{t_m}{S}\quad(S>s_1).
It is positive wherever t_m>0. Where S\le s_1\le a every term is non-negative, so the sum over the active indices is at most the sum over all indices, which is \lambda(k(a-S)+kS)=\Lambda. Where S>s_1 the sum over the active indices is at most \Lambda\sum_mt_m/S=\Lambda. On the fiber over t' with |t'|=\sigma\le\tau we have S=\sigma+u, and
\int_0^{s_1-\sigma}\frac{du}{\lambda(a-\sigma+(k-1)u)}+\int_{s_1-\sigma}^{L-\sigma}\frac{(\sigma+u)\,du}{\Lambda u}=\frac{W(\sigma)}{\Lambda}.
Lemma 13 with \Lambda=\sup W gives the inequality.

For the numerical statement it suffices to treat k=29. For \varepsilon\le143/1000, Proposition 14 gives M_{29,\varepsilon}\le1.143\cdot\frac{29}{28}\log29<3.9863. The interval [143/1000,1] is cut into 857 intervals [e,e'] of length 1/1000. For each one the file code/cert_floor_k29.json lists rational a and s_1 with \sup W<3.99 for (L,\tau)=(1+e',1-e); by monotonicity in L and \tau this covers every \varepsilon\in[e,e']. The supremum over \sigma is bounded rigorously: the first logarithm is non-increasing in \sigma (because a\ge s_1) and the last term is non-decreasing, so on a piece [x,y] we bound W by the first term at x plus the last at y, in ball arithmetic at rational points, bisecting until the bound is below 3.99. The script is code/dual_eps_cert.py; a second script, code/check_floor_cert.py, re-checks the certificate with a different interval library. The largest value of \sup W over the 857 intervals is 3.9772 (floating point). ◻

Remark 16 (what was known, and a caution). Polymath8b state the uniform bound M_{k,\varepsilon}\le\frac{k}{k-1}\log(2k-1) for 0\le\varepsilon<1 [14, Prop. 6.5], and remark that the bounds for M_{k,\varepsilon} “do not appear to be as sharp”. That bound is below 4 exactly for k\le23, so Theorem 15 moves this floor from 24 to 30. It does not use their bound. We note, with caution, that we could not complete the last step of the proof of [14, Prop. 6.5] as we read it: the weights 1-S+kt_m are summed, and for S>1 their sum over the active indices can exceed k (at t=(s,0,\dots,0) with 1<s\le1+\varepsilon only the first index is active and its weight is 1+(k-1)s). Our reconstruction gives the constant \frac{k}{k-1}+\varepsilon in place of \frac{k}{k-1}. For version 0.4 we re-read the proof in the current arXiv text: after the Cauchy–Schwarz step it says only “Integrating in t_1,\dots,t_{i-1}, t_{i+1},\dots,t_k and summing in i, we obtain the claim.” The weights 1-S+kt_i of the inactive indices can be negative when S>1, and dropping them is what lets the sum exceed k; it is at most k+(k-1)(S-1)\le k+(k-1)\varepsilon, which gives our constant. So the argument as printed proves M_{k,\varepsilon}\le\left(\frac{k}{k-1}+\varepsilon\right)\log(2k-1). Whether the stated inequality is nevertheless true is Open; we have not seen the journal version, and we may be missing an intended step. A brief search (the paper [14] and the Polymath wiki page on the variational problem) found no sharper published upper bound for M_{k,\varepsilon}; the search was not exhaustive, and Theorem 15 has not been reviewed by anyone outside this project.

Remark 17 (Computed, floating point only: how far this weight goes). The best value of \sup W over (a,s_1), maximised over \varepsilon, is about 3.975 for k=29, 4.004 for k=30, 4.257 for k=40 and 4.459 for k=50, in each case near \varepsilon=0.24 to 0.28; the bound of [14, Prop. 6.5] is 4.187, 4.218, 4.482 and 4.689. For small \varepsilon the two-zone weight does not beat the scaling bound: at k=50 and \varepsilon=1/25 both give 4.15 (4.1458 against 4.1515), while Polymath8b’s certified lower bound is 4.0043. The true threshold is presumably close to k=50, so the theorem is far from sharp. Script: code/dual_eps.py remark15.

Remark 18 (a weight that did not work). We also tried the weight \psi_m=\frac{\beta}{n}(a-S_A+nt_m), where n is the number of active indices at t and S_A the sum of the active coordinates. Its sum over the active indices is exactly \beta a everywhere, so the bound is a times the largest fiber integral. Evaluating the fiber integral exactly on the fibers over t'=(x,\dots,x,0,\dots,0) (Computed, floating point, k=50, \varepsilon=1/25, every a\in[1.05,3] on a grid of step 0.01, script code/dual_eps.py remark16) shows that this weight cannot give less than 4.178, which is worse than the 4.1515 from scaling. The binding fiber has a single non-zero coordinate x\approx2\varepsilon: there the zero coordinates become inactive while n is still large, and the normalisation by n wastes the slack. A dual bound that is sharper for small \varepsilon must treat the small coordinates near such sparse points differently. This is Open. (An earlier draft of this remark, version 0.3 as first circulated, reported a weaker obstruction, 4.146, from fibers with about ten non-zero coordinates; the exact fiber evaluation above supersedes it.)

The limit of weights that see only t_m and S (version 0.4)

Every weight used so far has the form \psi_m(t)=\Psi(S-t_m,t_m): Polymath8b’s \lambda(a-S+kt_m) and both zones of Theorem 15. Call this family \mathcal W. For weights in \mathcal W the problem “minimise the bound of Lemma 13” is convex: each fiber condition \int du/\Psi\le1 is convex in \Psi, and each point t gives a linear condition \sum_{m\text{ active}}\Psi(S-t_m,t_m)\le\Lambda. So its optimum can be computed, and it tells us how far any weight in \mathcal W, however clever, can go.

Proposition 19 (Computed, floating point, discretised; not a rigorous bound). Discretise \Psi=\rho\cdot(L-\sigma+(k-1)u) with \rho constant on cells of an N\times{}(geometric) grid in (S,u), impose the fiber conditions exactly on the grid \sigma\in\frac LN\mathbb Z, and impose the pointwise condition on all configurations of the grid of step L/N (found by a knapsack oracle). The convex optimum B_N(k,\varepsilon), computed by cutting planes with an exact convex solver at each step, is:

At \varepsilon=0 the optimum over the whole family is the classical weight: the solver, started anywhere, returns \frac{k}{k-1}\log k. At (50,1/25) the values have settled near 4.14: no weight that sees only t_m and S can bring the dual bound much below 4.14 there, while the true value is at least 4.0012. At (30,1/4) the discretisation has not converged (the values still fall, and lie above the continuum value \le4.0021 that Theorem 15 already certifies), so we draw no conclusion about the exact floor of \mathcal W; runs at k=31,32 gave B_{120}=4.0261 to 4.0976, also unconverged. The lesson is the first one: to approach the truth near k=50, a dual weight must depend on more of t than t_m and S. The optimal weight is known in principle: if F>0 is the extremal function, \psi_m=\int F\,dt_m/F makes Lemma 13 an equality (the Collatz–Wielandt principle for the positive operator F\mapsto\sum_{m\text{ active}}\int F\,dt_m). A first test (Computed, floating point, 20000 random points, not a bound; code/cw_sample.py) is encouraging on the plain simplex and discouraging on the enlarged one. For k=3, \varepsilon=0 and the degree-12 Galerkin function (M_3\ge1.646440) the sampled ratio stays in [1.6464,1.6465], which would be an almost sharp upper bound if the supremum could be certified. For \varepsilon>0 the Galerkin polynomial changes sign (\min F/\max F=-0.0003 at (k,\varepsilon,d)=(10,1/5,12), -0.11 at (50,1/25,12)). This is structural: where no index is active the extremal function vanishes, so near that region any positive F makes the ratio large. Making the principle usable there is Open. The script is code/dual_lp.py; runs/dual_lp_conv.log and runs/dual_lp_floor.log hold the output. The last column is from Table 2 and Theorem 6.

Remark 20 (heuristic: does the enlarged simplex stop at k=50?). The certified values of Table 2 grow with the degree roughly geometrically. Fitting M(D)=M_\infty-Cr^{D} to three consecutive even degrees (Aitken’s \Delta^2, script code/extrapolate_eps.py) can be tested where the answer is known. From the Computed values at D=16,18,20 for k=50, \varepsilon=1/25 it predicts 3.99324, 4.00195, 4.00553 at D=22,25,27, against the certified 3.99310 (degree 22), our 4.00125 (degree 25) and Polymath8b’s 4.0043 (degree 27). The model is slightly optimistic, by 0.0001 to 0.0012. Applied to k=49 with D=18,20,22 (certified 3.97715 and 3.97690 at degree 22 for \varepsilon=1/30 and 1/25) it gives limits 3.9922 and 3.9938. As a test registered before the run, the fit predicted 3.98230 at degree 24 for \varepsilon=1/30; the certified value is 3.98218 (1508 coefficients), again slightly below, and the fit through D=20,22,24 moves the limit down to 3.9913. The grid \varepsilon\in\{1/50,1/40,1/30,1/25,1/20\} at D\le20 gives at most 3.995. This suggests that \sup_\varepsilon M_{49,\varepsilon}<4, so that the \varepsilon-enlarged simplex with Bombieri–Vinogradov stops at k=50, consistent with the fact that every bound below 246 uses a different support. This is a heuristic, not a theorem: Open. Together with Theorem 15 it brackets the floor of this method between 30 and (conjecturally) 50.

Remark 21 (scope). Theorem 15 concerns the \varepsilon-enlarged simplex only. It says nothing about the 2026 claims, which use other supports and new equidistribution estimates. A floor for those is the Problem in the next section.

Programme

Track A: lowering the bound

  1. Climb the ladder. The certified pipeline now stands at k=50 (H_1\le246, the established bound, via the \varepsilon-enlarged simplex). Next: Stadlmann’s region (k=49, 240), which needs the rough-mass caps of [18] in the integration formulas.

  2. Floor of the method (our main original direction). For the plain simplex the dual bound M_k\le\frac{k}{k-1}\log k shows where the method stops (k\ge51). For the \varepsilon-enlarged simplex Theorem 15 gives k\ge30, which is still far from the expected truth near 50; Section 4 explains what a sharper weight must do, and Proposition 19 indicates that it must depend on more of t than t_m and S (a Collatz–Wielandt weight \psi_m=\int F\,dt_m/F built from a good positive test function F is the natural candidate). No upper bound of this kind is known to us for the 2026 hybrid supports. A rigorous dual certificate there would say whether 182 is close to the limit of the present inputs or far from it, and which input (which Type I/II/III range) is binding.

  3. Which rung is cheap. From Table 1, the gain per unit of k below the claimed frontier is 182\to176\to168\to162\to158 for k=39,\dots,35: the steps 39\to38 and 38\to37 are worth the most.

Problem 22 (Open). Give a rigorous upper bound for the variational quantity of [18] as a function of k and of the admissible exponents, and deduce the least k the hybrid method can reach with the currently proved estimates.

Track B: the parity barrier

No sieve of the above kind can distinguish integers with an even number of prime factors from those with an odd number. Known ways around the barrier all inject bilinear or structural information. We will study, one per session, what each would need in order to say something about twin primes: Chen’s theorem [4]; Bombieri’s asymptotic sieve [2]; the parity-breaking sieve of Friedlander and Iwaniec [6]; Heath-Brown’s theorem that Siegel zeros would imply the conjecture [7]; the function-field proof of Sawin and Shusterman [16]; and the entropy-based results on correlations of multiplicative functions [19].

Problem 23 (Open, to be made precise in Lean). State, as a single explicit hypothesis on bilinear sums over n(n+2), the weakest known input that implies the twin prime conjecture, and formalise the implication.
